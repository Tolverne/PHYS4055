====== PAGE 8 ======
Modelling an atom as a two-level system


 A real atom has many energy levels, but light close to one transition may interact
 predominantly with two of them:

                           |g⟩  ground state,      |e⟩  excited state.

                                                                        two level system
 Their energy separation defines the atomic transition frequency:

                             Ee −Eg = ℏωa.

 Choosing 𝐸𝑔= 0, the atomic Hamiltonian is
                          Ha = ℏωa|e⟩⟨e|.


 The two-level approximation is appropriate when other atomic transitions are sufficiently far
 from resonance.

The total excitation number is conserved


 Define the total excitation-number operator
                                 ˆNexc =                                 a†a + σ+σ−.

   It counts photons plus atomic excitations:
                                 ˆNexc|e, n⟩= (n + 1)|e, n⟩.

                            ˆNexc|g, n + 1⟩= (n + 1)|g, n + 1⟩.


  For the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian,

                                [HJC, ˆNexc] = 0.

 Therefore, the interaction can couple only states with the same total number of excitations:
                                        |e, n→←→|g, n + 1→.

 The state ∣𝑔, 0 ⟩ is uncoupled.

====== PAGE 20 ======
Why are they called dressed states?


 Without the interaction, the atom and field have separate bare states:

                                         |e, n⟩,      |g, n + 1⟩.


 When the interaction is included, these bare states combine to form the eigenstates of the complete
  atom–field system:

                                   n                                                |e, n⟩± |g, +                                                   1⟩ .                                |n, ±⟩=    √ 2


  •  The atom is said to be dressed by the electromagnetic field.

  •  The dressed states describe the atom and field together, rather than separately.

  •  The interaction changes both the states and their energies.

====== PAGE 23 ======
Vacuum Rabi oscillations

 Suppose the cavity initially contains no photons and the atom is excited:

                                 |ψ(0)⟩= |e, 0⟩.

 The interaction remains nonzero because
                               a†σ−|e, 0⟩= |g, 1⟩.
 The resulting evolution is

                         |ψ(t)⟩= cos(gt)|e, 0⟩−i sin(gt)|g, 1⟩.

 The corresponding probabilities are
                       Pe,0(t) = cos2(gt),     Pg,1(t) = sin2(gt).
  with vacuum Rabi angular frequency
                             Ω0 = 2g.


  •  The atom emits one photon into the initially empty cavity.
  •  The photon can subsequently be reabsorbed by the atom.
  •  The energy comes from the initially excited atom—not from the vacuum.

====== PAGE 24 ======
Coherent exchange versus spontaneous emission

 An ideal single-mode cavity produces reversible excitation exchange:
                                         Pe(t) = cos2(gt).

 For an atom emitting into many free-space modes, the excited-state population
 instead decays approximately as
                                     Pe(t) = e−Γt, where Γ is the spontaneous-emission rate.

           Ideal cavity                      Free space
        One selected field mode         A continuum of field modes
         The photon remains available       The photon propagates away
          Coherent reabsorption is possible    Reabsorption is unlikely
           Reversible oscillations              Approximately irreversible decay


  In a real cavity, vacuum Rabi oscillations are visible when the coherent coupling is stronger than
 the relevant losses:
                                g ≫κ, γ,

Vacuum Rabi splitting

 In the one-excitation manifold, the resonant dressed states are
                                                    |e, 0⟩± |g, 1⟩ .                                       |0, ±⟩=   √ 2
 Their energies are
                              E0,±                      = ℏωc ± ℏg.

 A weak probe can therefore excite the system at two frequencies:
                      =  ±                         ω±   ωc   g.

 The separation between these two resonances is
                         ω+ −ω−= 2g.

 This appearance of two spectral peaks is called vacuum Rabi splitting.

 Without coupling: one resonance at 𝜔𝑐.

 With strong coupling: two dressed-state resonances.

 The splitting is resolvable when it exceeds the spectral linewidths.                                                                     25

Jaynes–Cummings physics in a superconducting
qubit

 This experiment implements the same Jaynes–Cummings
 model in circuit QED

 It uses a superconducting qubit, often described as an
 artificial atom, and an on-chip superconducting microwave
 resonator

 Far off resonance, ∣Δ ∣≫𝑔, the spectrum is
 dominated by one cavity-like peak.

 On resonance, Δ = 0, resolvable strong coupling
 produces two dressed-state peaks separated by
 approximately 2g


  Wallraff, A., Schuster, D., Blais, A. et al. Strong coupling of a single photon to a superconducting qubit using circuit quantum electrodynamics.
  Nature 431, 162–167 (2004).

Summary

 One atom and one mode exchange excitations coherently

 The Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian describes a two-level atom coupled to one quantised cavity mode:
             HJC = ℏωca†a + ℏωaσ+σ−+ ℏg ! aσ+ + a†σ− " .


 Conservation of total excitation number divides the system into coupled pairs:
                               √                            n                       +      Ωn = 2g                                            n                                 |e, n→←→|g,     1→,          + 1.


 The interaction creates dressed atom–field states, producing Rabi oscillations
 and a measurable energy splitting. For the vacuum field,

                               |e, 0→←→|g, 1→,    ω+ −ω−= 2g.
