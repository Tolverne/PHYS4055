====== PAGE 6 ======
Photodetection

•  Most photodetectors apply a version of the photoelectric effect to
   operate

•   Radiation ionizes a piece of photosensitive material and produces
    freely moving electrons

•   This produces an electric current (photocurrent) that can be amplified

   and handled by electronic means

•   Conventional photodiodes: A photon creates an electron-hole pair in
   the region between the p-doped and n-doped layers such that a
    current can flow. This photocurrent is proportional to the photon flux.                               Silberhorn, C. (2007). Detecting quantum light.
                                                                                               Contemporary Physics, 48(3), 143–156
•  Because of noise from thermal excitations, conventional
   photodiodes cannot be used for the detection of single photons

Avalanche photodiodes

     •   Single photons can be detected through avalanche

      photodiodes (APD)


     •  A single electron is typically liberated by a single photon


     •  High voltage is applied across a so-called ‘multiplication

        region’ of the diode


     •  Charge carriers are amplified to macroscopic current pulses.                                                                                                                       p. 242 Bachor, Hans-A. / Ralph, Timothy C.
                                                                          A guide to experiments in quantum optics (2019)
     •  A single photon triggers an avalanche of charge carriers

Photon number resolving detectors

•   Ideal photon number resolving detectors implement projection on individual
   Fock states
                Πn = |n⟩⟨n|

•  Need physical processes that have a measurable response to extremely low
   changes in intensity of incident light

•   Superconducting-nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPD) are used as photon
   number resolving detectors

•  A photon is absorbed in a superconducting nanowire, the photon energy is
    transferred to electrons and phonons

•   This energy produces a small non-superconducting region known as a “hot spot”
   and the wire transitions to a resistive state
