1 EM ﬁeld as a set of quantum harmonic oscil-
    lators

Further reading: M&S13.1-2

To go beyond the semiclassical approach, we need to quantize the electromagnetic
ﬁeld itself. As we shall see, the concept of photons appears naturally (and un-
avoidably). We will also ﬁnd that, as for the quantum mechanics of particles, so
for quantum ﬁelds there are canonically conjugate variables whose simultaneous
values cannot be predicted with absolute certainty. In other words, the quantized
light has an irreducible uncertainty, or quantum noise.
   For example, in quantum mechanics, the position and momentum of a particle
are incompatible observables, represented by noncommuting operators. In electro-
magnetism, the electric ﬁeld is determined through the placement of a test charge,
whereas to determine the magnetic ﬁeld, we also need to know the velocity of the
test charge: F = q(E + v ⇥B). So it is no surprise to ﬁnd out that we cannot de-termine both the electric and magnetic ﬁeld amplitudes exactly, and that there are
some situations where E and B behave like conjugate variables, with an associated
uncertainty relation.
   Given that light is an oscillatory (wave) solution of Maxwell’s equations, it is
not surprising that the route to quantising an optical ﬁeld is to treat is as a set
of harmonic oscillators. The general approach will be to determine the oscillating
modes in the classical problem, express each mode in the form of a harmonic
oscillator, and then quantise it accordingly.
  We begin by quantise a single standing wave in a cavity before treating the
more general, multimode problem

2  Coherent states

Further reading: M&S13.4

   Having formally introduced the photon, we now look at its role in two very
useful kinds of quantum states: thermal (or chaotic) states and coherent states.
These states have a classical interpretation, but quantum formulation give us a
‘photon’ description that combines the wave and particle pictures.
   In the following we will just consider the state of a single-mode ﬁeld (which
could be e.g. a standing wave in a cavity, or a propagating plane wave in free
space), but the results are easily generalised to the multimode situation.

2.1  Quadratures and phase space

Coherent states have both a physical signiﬁcance (as fully coherent light, or an
idealisation of laser output) and a mathematical utility (as a basis set for rep-
resenting a general quantum state). They were ﬁrst constructed by Schr¨odinger
in 1926, and ever since Glauber utilized them in 1963 in his quantum theory of
coherence, they have played a central role in quantum optics.
    Classically, a coherent state is characterised by a well deﬁned amplitude E.
In quantum mechanics, the uncertainty principle prohibits us from specifying E
exactly, because the real and imaginary parts of E correspond to noncommutingoperators, but we can deﬁne a state that has an amplitude with the smallest
variance possible. This smallest variance is the uncertainty of the vacuum state.
   To quantify these statements, we introduce the quadrature operators:

                       X1 =  (a† + a)
                       X2 =  i(a† −a),                           (32)
which, up to a constant factor, are just the ‘position’ and ‘momentum’ operators
introduced earlier in the Hamiltonian formulation2 (see Eq. (9)). Classically, X1
and X2 constitute a phase space, in which the system dynamics can be mapped
out with deterministic trajectories. Quantumly, the state at a particular time can-
not be speciﬁed by a single point in phase-space, due to quantum indeterminacy.
Instead, a quantum state can be thought of as a distribution of points in phase
space, centred at (hX1i, hX2i) and characterised by the variances var(X1), var(X2).
   2For a standing wave in a cavity and with the appropriate choice of phase, hX1i is proportional

and magnetic ﬁelds oscillate in phase. In this case, hX1i gives the cos Kz contribution to the
electric ﬁeld and hX2i gives the sin Kz contribution

5.1  Squeezing

Up until now, the only truly nonclassical states we have described have been the
number states, and although there has been much progress in recent years with
single-photon sources, number states are very challenging to produce reliably. A
common alternative to achieving nonclassical behaviour in light has been through
squeezing, in which the uncertainty in one variable is ‘squeezed’ below the coherent-
state level. For quadratures (deﬁned in Eq. (32)), this means
                   h∆X21i < 1,  or h∆X22i < 1,                     (100)
but not both simultaneously, which would violate Heisenberg’s uncertainty princi-
ple. Besides revealing nonclassical behaviour, such as antibunching and subpois-
sonian statistics, squeezed states are of interest as a route to generating entangled
states and for practical application in reducing noise in interferometric and other
kinds of precision measurements.
   The ideal squeezed state is, like the coherent state, a minimum-uncertainty
state. But its ‘noise distribution’ in phase space is not symmetric - it is an ellipse

5.3  How to generate squeezing

5.3.1   χ(2) nonlinear process

Squeezing is typically generated through the use of some nonlinear medium. A
medium with a χ(2) nonlinear polarisation response can be used to parametrically
amplify one quadrature and ‘de-amplify’ another to create squeezing. In particular,
a pump of frequency 2⌦can be used to create a squeezed vacuum of frequency ⌦.
The e↵ective hamiltonian is

                               +          ,             (119)                       ˆHχ(2) = ~⌦a†a + 2~⌦c†c + ~χ  ca†2                                   ⇣      c†a2⌘
which describes the process of one c photon (from the pump) begin converted into
2 a photons (and vice versa)10. If the pump ﬁeld has a large intensity, it is often a
good approximation to represented it as a constant classical ﬁeld (the ‘undepleted
pump approximation’), in which case the unitary operator describing the evolution
in the interaction picture has the same form as the squeezing operator, Eq. (102).
One insight that this gives us is that squeezing is linked to the creation of photons
in pairs.

6  Quasiprobability distributions

It is fairly common to represent coherent, squeezed and other quantum states

deviations. Such representations can be given rigour by deﬁning distributions using
coherent states. As we shall see, although any quantum state can be represented
by some sort of distribution in phase space, there are some subtleties over how
such a distribution is to be interpreted.
